The Lake Is Still

A novella of a Georgia school year

Walter Hayes has taught mathematics in the same classroom in Gainesville, Georgia, for nineteen of his thirty years. His first-period students arrive the way tired adults arrive anywhere — many of them coming off night shifts at the plants and warehouses around town, with the hours left over for sleep.

This is the story of one school year: of a teacher who stops writing one referral, starts watching one quiet girl in the third row, and learns what his students’ lives actually look like outside his room.

Chapter One — September

First period was the quietest class of Walter Hayes's day, because half of them were not entirely awake.

They came in the way they always came in, in twos and threes, hoods up, earbuds in, carrying gas-station coffee and breakfast biscuits wrapped in foil. Room 214 faced the back of the school, where the ground sloped down past the practice field to the lake, and on September mornings the water sat behind the glass in a flat gray sheet, barely brighter than the sky. Walt had taught in this room for nineteen of his thirty years. He had watched the school change around it — the portable classrooms out by the bus loop, the new wing, the Spanish on the hallway posters going from a few words to whole sentences — but the window had not changed, and neither had the first ten minutes of first period.

He took attendance on the screen, the way the district required, and then he walked the rows, the way he required of himself.

"Up we go," he said, and tapped a desk with two fingers. "Morning, DeShawn."

DeShawn lifted his head off his folded arms, slow as a man surfacing. "Morning, Mr. Hayes."

"Long night?"

"Work."

"Work," Walt said, and moved on, because in this room that was not an excuse. It was a schedule. The chicken plants ran their shifts and the fast-food places on the bypass ran theirs, and his students ran on the hours left over. Some of them closed. Some of them stocked. Some of them stood in the cold at the end of a line he did not let himself picture too closely. They came to first period the way tired adults go to anything — because it was where they were supposed to be — and they slept with their heads down in the braced, shallow way of people who expect to be woken.

By long habit, Walt woke them gently and early, in the first ten minutes, before the lesson. It was a rule he had made in some year he could no longer name: nobody got written up for sleeping in Room 214, and nobody got to sleep through it either. He woke them and they forgave him, mostly, and the day went on.

Marisol Vega did not sleep.

She sat in the third row by the window, a senior in a class of seniors, and she held her pen from the moment she sat down, as if putting it down were the first step in a fall she intended not to take. She was eighteen, small-boned, with her hair pulled back tight, and she had the stillness of a person spending something. Other students drifted and surfaced. Marisol stayed at the surface by effort, the way a swimmer treads water — calmly, without splashing, at a cost that only showed if you watched her for a whole period, which Walt, that September, began to do.

She never once asked him for anything. That was how he noticed her, in the end. In thirty years he had learned that the students who asked for nothing were carrying the most, and that they asked with other parts of themselves — with attendance, with punctuality, with work turned in at 11:58 p.m. the night before it was due. Marisol's homework arrived past the midnight deadline, at hours his phone showed him in the morning: 2:14 a.m., 3:40 a.m. He told himself it was a phone habit, the way teenagers lived now. He almost believed it.

Ethan Cole sat in the front row, where he had sat since August, and everything about Ethan was arranged. His notebook was open before the bell. He underlined twice — once in the writing, once in the checking — and his answers came up clean and straight, one line following the next like fence posts. He was first in the class and he carried it the way some boys carry a family name: not as a pleasure, but as an assignment. Twice that month Walt had seen him erase a correct answer and write it again, smaller, as if the first version had been too visible.

Marcus Webb sat in the back corner and did everything at the wrong speed. He finished quizzes in half the time and put his head down for the rest, and his papers were strange in a way that took Walt most of September to name. The work wandered. It started problems the long way, crossed out, backed up, arrived sideways. And on every test there were gaps — not scattered, not the pattern of a boy who had run out of knowledge, but placed, like stones skipped on purpose. The hardest question on the second quiz, a question only three students in four classes had solved, Marcus had solved on his scratch paper and left blank on the answer sheet.

Walt had seen boys hide being smart before. It was never about the smartness. It was about what the smartness would cost at home, or in the hallway, or in the mirror. He put the observation in the drawer of his mind where he kept such things, to be asked about later, in the right week, in the right way. Thirty years teaches a man which questions keep.

The midterm came in the last week of September, and Walt graded it on Sunday afternoon at his kitchen table, the way he had graded midterms since before his students' parents were born.

Carol was making soup. The radio was on low. Paper by paper, the class went by under his pen — the strong ones, the lost ones, the ones who had guessed with style — and then he put down Ethan Cole's test and picked up Marcus Webb's, and the room changed temperature.

Question 12. A rate problem, two steps, the kind that sorts a class into the careful and the quick. The answer was 4.37.

Both boys had written 4.73.

Walt sat back. A transposed answer was nothing — half the class transposed something, it was the most ordinary error in the world. But he had graded Ethan's paper first and frowned at it without knowing why, and now he knew why. He laid the two tests side by side on the table, under the kitchen light.

Marcus's work for Question 12 wandered the way all his work wandered: a wrong start, a crossing-out, a small correct wilderness of arithmetic, and at the end of it, 4.73 — wrong, but honestly wrong, the error made early and carried faithfully to the end, the way a person carries a wrong turn all the way to the wrong street.

Ethan's work for Question 12 was clean. Every line followed. Every line was right. And if you followed those right lines to where they actually went, they arrived at 4.37.

His answer said 4.73.

A boy who solves a problem gets the answer his work produces. A boy who copies gets somebody else's — and if the somebody else was wrong, he gets the wrongness too, gift-wrapped in somebody else's handwriting. Ethan's paper was a clean road built to a house that stood on a different street.

Walt looked at the two tests for a long time. The soup made its small sounds on the stove. Somewhere behind his eyes, thirty years of boys filed past, and he did the arithmetic teachers do and never write down: what a referral would cost each of them. The district's policy was not ambiguous. Suspected academic dishonesty went to Ms. Doyle's office on a form, in triplicate, and both students were investigated, and both files were marked — both, regardless of which end of the pencil the truth was on. The machine did not ask why a boy would copy, or why another boy would let him. The machine asked what happened, and stopped asking there.

"You've been at that one a while," Carol said.

"Two papers," Walt said. "Same wrong answer."

"Cheating?"

"Somebody is," he said. "That's the trouble. I'm not sure it's the somebody everyone will think."

She set a bowl down beside the tests, where there was room, and did not say anything else, because she had been married to him for thirty-one years and knew the sound of a man doing long division in his head.

On Monday he came in at 6:40, as he always came in, and made the coffee in the teachers' lounge, and went to Room 214 and stood a moment in the empty room with the windows full of lake. Then he sat at his desk, took a referral form from the drawer, and began to write.

Name. Date. Period.

Third line: Description of incident.

His pen stopped above the line. It stayed there while the first buses came around the loop and the hallway filled with the noise a school makes when it is waking up — lockers, laughter, somebody's music escaping somebody's phone. He thought about Ethan's erased answers, written again smaller. He thought about Marcus's blank space where a right answer had been proven on scratch paper and withheld, by its owner, on purpose. He thought about the machine in Ms. Doyle's office, which would ask what happened and stop asking there, and he thought about what he would be if he fed two boys into it to keep his own record clean.

He put the form back in the drawer, under the spare pencils, and closed the drawer, and got up to teach first period.

It was not a decision, he told himself, going to the door. It was only a delay. But he had been a teacher long enough to know that a delay is a decision too — just one a man makes with his eyes open.

The class came in, in twos and threes, hoods up, foil-wrapped biscuits in hand. Marisol Vega took her seat by the window and picked up her pen. Past the rows of bent heads, past the practice field, the lake lay flat and bright and entirely still.

Chapter Two — October

By the second week of October, Marisol Vega had been late to first period three times.

Three lates was a line in the school's rules, and past the line the machinery started: a letter home, a meeting with a counselor, a note in the first-period teacher's box. Walt found the note on a Tuesday. Vega, Marisol — 12th — tardy conference, Thursday, 3:30. Please attend if possible. He read it twice, and then he pulled up the attendance screen and looked at the three small marks beside her name, and each of them was no more than six minutes wide. Six minutes, three times. She was running a life with no slack in it, and the slack was going six minutes at a time.

She was never late by more, and that was the thing he could not stop seeing. Students who did not care arrived twenty minutes late, an hour late, a whole morning gone with a shrug. Marisol was late the way a full cup spills — a little, at the rim, no matter how steady the hand that carries it.

On Thursday he went to the conference.

The counselor's office was a small room with a plant that refused to die and a box of tissues that never emptied. Ms. Okafor had the attendance record open on her screen and a kind, worn way of asking questions that Walt recognized, because it was his own. Marisol sat in the chair nearest the door. Her pen was in her hand. He noticed that, and then noticed that he noticed it in every room she entered, as if the pen were a rail she held on stairs only she could see.

"Marisol," Ms. Okafor said, "we just want to understand the mornings. Three times now. Is something going on at home?"

"No, ma'am."

"Is the bus a problem?"

"I drive."

"Car trouble?"

"No, ma'am."

The answers came short and clean and closed, like doors shut politely, one after another, down a hall. Ms. Okafor looked at Walt over the girl's head, the look adults trade when the script has run out. And Walt felt the end of the meeting already in his mouth — the promise, the signatures, everyone standing — because thirty years of such meetings had taught him exactly how they ended, and the teaching had made him tired in a place he did not usually look at.

So instead he said a thing that was not in the script. "What time do you get home from work, Marisol?"

The pen stopped moving in her hand. That was the only sign she gave. She did not startle, and she did not look away. She simply answered the question that had actually been asked, in the same clean voice.

"Four. A little after."

"In the morning."

"Yes, sir."

Ms. Okafor's pen stopped too. "You work until four in the morning?"

"I work sanitation," Marisol said. "At the plant. Ten to four, Sunday to Thursday. Friday and Saturday till two."

The room went quiet. Down the hall a locker slammed and a boy laughed, far off, in the world where school was the whole of the day — and Walt sat in the small room and understood, with the particular clarity that shame brings, that he had been teaching this girl for six weeks without knowing the first true thing about her schedule, and that the three little marks beside her name had been telling him all along, in the only language the school kept.

Then, because Ms. Okafor was good at her work and asked the next questions gently, and because Marisol — he saw this now, and kept it — hid nothing and volunteered nothing, answered only what was truly asked, the whole shape of the girl's day came out onto the table between them, hour by hour, in numbers, like a proof laid out line by line on his own board.

The shift ended at four. She drove home on roads given over to plant traffic and trucks, in the last of the dark. She slept from five until six-thirty, in the room she shared with her little sister, a towel hung over the window against a sun that rose without consulting her. At six-thirty she rose and made breakfast and got the small ones up and dressed and fed — a brother of nine, a sister of six — because her mother left for the plant's first shift at five and her father at five-thirty, and a house, like a line, runs because somebody stands in the gap. She drove the children to their school. She drove herself to hers. First period began at 7:45.

After school she slept again, five to nine, in the same room, under the same towel, while the family's evening went on around her behind the door — her mother's cooking, her father's television, her brother's whole loud life, held down, she did not need to say, to a hush the size of love. Then she rose into the dusk and ate what had been left for her on the stove, and helped her sister with her bath, and read to her brother if there was time, and drove across town to the plant, where the work was water and cold: hoses that kicked like living things, foam, the opened insides of great machines that had to be made cleaner than clean by morning, because food passed through them and strangers' children ate. Boots that stayed wet. A coat that stayed cold. A sharp, clean chemical smell that lived in her hair for days, so that on Monday mornings, in the third row, if the room was warm and the window shut, Walt could sometimes catch it — soap, and underneath it something that soap was for.

"And homework," Ms. Okafor said at last.

"After my shift," Marisol said. "Or before. It gets done."

2:14 a.m. 3:40 a.m. The times glowed in his memory like numbers on a clock in a dark room. She was not failing. Her grades stood at B's, with an A in his own class; she was not lost, or in trouble, or in need of rescue by anyone. She was running one full life in the light and another in the dark, and the two lives met for perhaps four hours a day, in pieces, behind a towel over a window — and out of those pieces she had assembled, without complaint and without announcement, an education.

The conference ended as conferences end. There was a form, and the form said that the student had been counseled on the importance of punctuality. Ms. Okafor signed it. Marisol signed it. Walt signed it last, and his own signature, under theirs, looked to him like a word written correctly in a language he was no longer sure he spoke.

Two weeks later came the second meeting, the ordinary business of a senior year — credits, the spring schedule — with her parents this time. Her father had his plant badge clipped at his belt, the way a man who works with his hands forgets such a thing is on him. Her mother held her purse in her lap with both hands and looked at the room the way people look at offices that belong to their children's other world: with respect, and with the wish not to touch anything.

The school's Spanish interpreter was out that day. So Marisol interpreted.

Walt had seen it done before, in other offices, by other children, and it undid him every time in the same place. Her mother would speak — a full sentence, round and warm, with her hands moving inside it — and Marisol would catch it and carry it across and set it down in English: smaller, flatter, the edges trimmed, the way you carry something hot in a thin towel. She says the spring schedule is fine. She says thank you for watching out for me. Then Ms. Okafor would answer, and the girl would carry that back, and he could watch it grow in Spanish again toward the shape her mother had made, the warmth coming back into it as it approached home. The words went through the daughter like water through a narrow place, and came out the other side changed in pressure, and everyone in the room pretended this was a normal way for a family to talk to a school, because it was. It was entirely normal. That was the thing. It was normal the way the lake was still: because it happened every day, and no one had ever once been able to make it happen any other way.

Near the end, her mother said something longer. She said it looking at Walt, and she touched her chest once, lightly, in the middle of it, as if to keep the sentence — or herself — in place. Marisol listened. And for the first time in either meeting, before she carried the words across, she paused.

"She says I was always a good student," Marisol said. "She says they're sorry about the mornings. The car is old. She says it isn't the work. It's the car."

Her mother's hands stayed in her lap. Her father studied his boots, which were clean and old. No one in the room asked for pity, and no one performed endurance; they were telling a teacher about a car, in an office, on a Thursday, and it was all ordinary and all true, and the ordinariness of it settled in Walt's chest like a stone carried in a pocket — not heavy enough to stop a man walking, too present, ever, to pretend it was not there.

He did what he could do. It was small, and he knew it was small, and he did it so that it would belong to everyone.

Homework for Room 214 had been due at midnight since the first year of the online system. That Sunday night, at his kitchen table, Walt changed the setting. Work for his classes would be due at 7:30 in the morning, before first period. He announced it to his classes on Monday as a plain fact, no reason attached, and in two rooms a few students cheered, because the young hear the word later and applaud before they think; and he let them, because joy at that hour of the morning was rare enough to leave alone. For most of them the change was nothing. For one girl in the third row it moved her schoolwork out of the middle of her working night and set it at the edge of her own morning — out of the plant's hours and into hers. She never thanked him, and he found, examining it honestly, that he was glad she didn't; gratitude would have made it a gift, and it was not a gift. It was a fence post moved three inches on a boundary. The times on his phone began to change. 11:48. 11:36. Once, on a Sunday, 9:15 in the evening — practically daylight, for her. Practically rest.

He did not feel virtuous about it, and would have distrusted the feeling if it had come. Thirty years he had believed that what a classroom offered was knowledge, and fairness, and a door kept open; and he was learning now — late, from the wrong direction, the way men learn the things that matter — that sometimes the whole of it was three inches of ground, given quietly, and that giving it anyway was not nothing. It was not nothing. He turned that sentence over more than once in the weeks that followed, testing it, the way you test a step in the dark before you put your weight down.

October ended warm. On the last Friday of the month he left by the long way, past the practice field, at the hour when the light goes. The sun was setting red over the lake, and on the benches by the shore where the students gathered at lunch there was no one at all — the water taking the color, the field emptying, the whole world of the school exhaling into the weekend. The lake lay open and bright and still, keeping its own counsel, as it had kept it every day of his thirty years, asking nothing of the town that had risen on its shore.

That night at 9:40, across town, in a parking lot lit white as an operating room, Marisol Vega would badge in for the sanitation shift — hair tied back, boots already waiting by her locker, her school pen left behind in Room 214, in the desk she would return to at 7:45 on Monday morning, in the other half of her life.
