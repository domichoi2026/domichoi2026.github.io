While Waiting

Six linked stories of one night terminal

Atlanta Central, on Forsyth Street, is open all night. Buses come and go at hours when the rest of the city is asleep, and the people in the waiting room are there for reasons that rarely fit on a ticket. While Waiting is a collection of six linked stories set in that room, across its nights.

The stories are connected by Earl, 68, the night clerk, who keeps the night's buses on a handwritten clipboard and appears, briefly, in each of them. Each story turns on waiting — not on what finally arrives, but on one small decision a person makes while they wait, and on how that decision touches the next person's night.

The first two stories are here, to read and to listen to. The other four are not posted; they will appear with the full collection, which is forthcoming.

Story One — The 10:25

The bus stop in Gainesville was a Shell station on Monroe Drive. There was no terminal and no sign for one. There was a gas station with a bright lot, and a place at the side of the lot where the bus pulled in, and if you didn't already know that was the stop, nobody was going to tell you.

Maya's mother had parked facing the street so they could watch for it. The bus was due at 10:25. It was 10:31 by the dashboard clock, and her mother had the engine running for the heat, though it wasn't cold enough yet to need it. It was the end of October. Maya wore a hoodie over her shirt and the zipper was up.

"You have the ticket?" her mother said.

"It's on my phone," Maya said. "It's just a code. I show him the code."

She had bought it on the app two days before, $12.48, Gainesville to Atlanta. She had a duffel that would go under the bus and a small bag she would carry on, and a plastic grocery bag with a sandwich and two boiled eggs and a bottle of sweet tea that her mother had packed after supper, as if Atlanta were farther than fifty-five miles.

The job started Monday. It was in the billing office of a freight company out by the airport, answering phones and entering invoices into a computer, $17 an hour, which was three and a half dollars more than she made at the grocery store. Dre's friend from high school worked in that office and had put her name in. Dre was her cousin. He had a room in a house on Peters Street, on the other side of the stadium lots from the bus station, and he was going to pick her up when she got in and let her sleep on his couch until her first paycheck. Then she would find a room of her own. That was the whole plan, and it fit in one sentence, and her mother had repeated it to the neighbors as if it were already done.

Maya had not wanted the job. The pay was better and her mother was proud, and those were the two reasons she was sitting in a gas station lot at night with her bags. Her own reason had not shown up yet. She was 24. She told herself it might show up in Atlanta.

The bus came at 10:41, big and lit, and swung wide through the lot. She showed the driver her phone, and he looked at the code and nodded. He opened the baggage bay and stood back, because putting the bag in was her job. The duffel was heavier than she expected and she got it in on the second push. Her mother had gotten out of the car. She hugged Maya in the light of the pumps with the bus idling beside them.

"Text me when you get there, mija," she said.

"I will."

Rain started somewhere below Gainesville, fine and straight down, and by the time the bus reached the interstate it was running off the windows in sheets. Maya texted Dre: on the bus. He answered: ok. She watched the dark go by — the exits for the warehouses along I-85, all lit up and working, trucks backed up to the doors at midnight like the day had never ended — and then the city beginning, and the bus slowing, and turning, and turning again.

The bus was half full. Across the aisle a man slept sitting straight up with his bag on his lap and his hand through the strap. The network name for the bus's Wi-Fi came up on her phone, and she connected, and nothing loaded, and after a while she stopped trying. She thought about Monday instead: an office she had seen once, on the interview, in a picture Dre's friend had texted her — rows of desks, a window that looked at a parking lot and the airport fence beyond it. Invoices. Phones. She had worn her good blouse for the picture, though nobody had asked her to. She thought about her boxes at her mother's house — two of them, in the corner of her old room, with her winter clothes and her high school things, to be sent for later, when there was a place. She thought about the word later, which had carried a lot of her life so far.

Atlanta at 11:52 at night. The station was on Forsyth Street, a long low building with rows of connected seats and vending machines against one wall and a security guard by the door who looked at her bags and then didn't. She hauled her duffel out of the bay herself, the way everyone did, and stood a minute in the wet light of the bus lane getting her bearings. Then she went inside and texted Dre: I'm here.

The text said Delivered. Then it said Read.

She texted her mother too — I'm here — because she had promised, and because it was true, even if it wasn't the whole truth. Her mother would be asleep with the phone on the kitchen counter, and would find it in the morning and worry at exactly the level the words allowed.

For the first half hour Maya stood out front under the station's overhang with her duffel at her feet, watching the cars come down Forsyth. A taxi slowed and went on. A car with a cracked windshield slowed and went on. A man came out of the station with a trash bag of clothes over his shoulder, looked both ways once, and set off walking into the wet dark like he knew every step of it. The guard came to the door and said, not unkindly, "You can wait inside where it's warm, baby. He'll have to come to the door for you anyway." So she went back in.

She sat where she could see the front doors. People came and went. A man in a plant uniform slept with his head back and his mouth open and his ticket in his hand so he wouldn't have to dig for it. A woman with a tote bag had spread out over two seats with a folder on her chest, asleep or nearly. At 12:20 Maya called Dre. It rang until it stopped. At 12:40 she called again and got the same.

"Pickup's out front on Forsyth," somebody said.

It was the night clerk. He was tall and thin and older than her father would have been, with a clipboard under his arm and a name tag that said EARL. He had a way of saying things to the room in general, not to anyone in particular, like the information belonged to whoever needed it.

"He might be parked around the side," Earl said. "Some of them circle. If you're going to sit a while, sit by the back wall. The outlets back there work. The ones by the machines don't."

"Thank you," Maya said.

She moved to the back wall and plugged in her phone, which was at 38 percent. She sat with her duffel between her feet and her carry-on on her knees and her mother's grocery bag on the seat beside her, next to the umbrella she had hung over the arm of the bench to dry. It was her mother's umbrella, yellow, the cheerful kind you buy at Dollar General for somebody you love and expect to lose. It had been raining in Gainesville when they left. It was raining here too, lightly, on and off.

She ate the sandwich at one. She drank half the sweet tea. Sometime after that she typed a text to her mother — Dre fell asleep, I'm still at the station, I'm fine — and sat looking at it. It was all true. It would also put her mother in the car on I-985 in the rain inside of twenty minutes, and her mother driving at night on the interstate, half asleep and worried, was a worse arithmetic than this room. Maya deleted it letter by letter. She would tell her in the morning, on the phone, in her own voice, when there was a couch and a Monday already underneath her. What she sent instead, she would decide later.

At two she bought a coffee from the machine, which came out pale and very hot, and she held it with both hands because a room you sit in all night gets cold in a way a room you walk through doesn't. Her hoodie was zipped to her chin. Across the room the guard changed his position from one door to the other. Earl went down the rows with a mop once, not because anything had spilled, as far as she could tell, but because it was two o'clock and that was when he did it. He checked his clipboard against the board. Everything on the board was also on her phone, but Earl had written the night's buses out by hand in pencil, the cities and the times, in a column as neat as a grocery list.

Around twenty to three, a bus boarded for Jacksonville. The ceiling said something muffled, and the people it belonged to stood up anyway, the way you learn a place's sounds by sitting in it: a young couple with a baby asleep in a carrier, an old man carrying a car-seat bag, two girls sharing one pair of earbuds who got in line still sharing them. Earl read the driver's paperwork at the gate and wrote one more line on his clipboard, and the room went back to its handful of sleepers, thinner than before.

At 3:15 a bus came in from New York, and the room changed. People got up from everywhere, from the seats and the walls, and there was a line at the restroom and a line at the machines, and the guard stood in the middle of it with his hands behind his back, and for ten minutes the station was a busy place in the middle of the night. The woman with the tote bag woke and sat up and held her folder in her lap.

"Would you watch this a minute?" she said to Maya. "I hate to carry it all in there."

"Yes ma'am," Maya said.

The woman went to the restroom. Maya sat with the tote bag at her feet next to her own duffel and watched both of them, and it came to her that this was the main business of the whole room: watching things for people, your own and other people's, while everybody waited for the thing that was coming to come.

"Where's yours going?" the woman said, when she came back and sat down again.

"This was mine," Maya said. "I got in at midnight. I'm waiting on my cousin. He's supposed to pick me up."

The woman nodded like that was a complete story, which it was. "I have an appointment at the hospital at eight," she said. "A heart doctor. It took three months to get it. This bus was the only one that got me here in time. So I sit." She said it without complaint. "Fourth time I've made this trip. The sitting is part of the appointment, far as I can tell." She had a way of arranging herself — folder, bag, coat folded under her head before long — like a person who had done this sitting before and knew its length.

A gate announcement went out sometime after four, muffled, in the ceiling, and Maya couldn't make out a word of it. It wasn't for her. None of them were for her. Her bus had already come.

At 4:47 her phone lit up. Dre: fell asleep. u good?

She read it twice. She set the phone face down on her knee.

The station had gone quiet again. The New York people had dispersed into the city on other buses and in cars that came for them out front, headlights sweeping the doors. The man in the plant uniform was gone. The woman with the folder was asleep with her head on her folded coat. Earl was at the far end of the room, turning his clipboard over, writing something on the back of it in pencil.

"Dre's place is four blocks past the stadium," Maya said, mostly to herself, but Earl heard her, because the room was that quiet.

"Peters Street?" he said.

"Yes sir."

"Daylight makes it a different walk," Earl said.

She had had the address all night. It was in her phone, in the messages, 18 minutes by the walking directions. She had not gone because it was dark, and she didn't know the streets, and she had two bags, and a woman alone at 1 a.m. in a city she had only ever driven through does the arithmetic the city taught her. All of that was true. It was also true that Dre had said he would come, and she had been waiting to be fetched the way you are fetched when other people are still arranging your life. Both things had kept her in the seat. Now the light outside the front doors had gone from black to the grey that comes before blue, and the rain had stopped sometime before five, and the arithmetic was different.

She went to the restroom and washed her face in water that ran cold a long time before it ran warm. In the mirror she looked like somebody who had sat up all night, which she had. She dried her face and re-did her ponytail and put the hood of her sweatshirt down. When she came out, the first bus of the morning was boarding at one of the gates — Charlotte, the board said — and people were moving toward it in the ordinary way, with their bags and their coffees, like the night had been a waiting room and nothing more. A girl about her age with a backpack went out the front doors alone, unhurried, and turned up Forsyth and was gone.

She stood up. She put the carry-on strap over her shoulder and took the duffel by its handles and set the empty grocery bag inside the duffel's side pocket, because her mother would have wanted the bag kept. Her phone, charged and bright, said 6:04.

The umbrella was hanging where she had left it at midnight, over the arm of the bench, still a little damp. She took it down and held it a moment. Her hands were full, which was the plain truth. She hung it back.

It would dry there. Somebody would need it before she did.

Earl had come up to the front with his clipboard, the way he must have done for every departure and every arrival, whether anyone was watching or not. He held the door for her with the hand that wasn't holding the board.

"You take care now," he said.

"Yes sir," Maya said. "Thank you."

Forsyth Street was wet and getting light, and the pavement shone. A train went over somewhere behind the station, on the Garnett line, starting its day. Maya turned the way the phone told her — toward the stadium lots, toward Peters Street, four blocks and then a little more — and started walking.

Story Two — Tuesdays

Harold came in at eleven, the way he came in every Tuesday, with his paperback in the pocket of his jacket and nothing else to carry. He was 79. He had driven a bread truck for twenty-six years, out of a bakery warehouse on the south side, starting his routes at hours most people called the middle of the night, so the dark had never been a foreign country to him. He parked his Camry in the lot beside the station, in the third space from the light, and he came through the doors on Forsyth Street and nodded to the guard, who nodded back the way you nod at weather you have already checked.

His seat was in the third row, at the end, facing the gates. It was not his seat in any way the station knew about. It was his seat because he had sat in it for a hundred and some Tuesdays, and because from it he could see the doors and the gate counter and the place where the arriving buses let their people off, all without turning his head much. A man watching for somebody learns to arrange the room so the room does the work.

"Evening, Mr. Harold," Earl said, passing with his clipboard.

"Earl."

"Charlotte's running forty behind, if you're keeping track."

"I'll write it down," Harold said, and Earl went on down the rows, and that was the whole of it, most weeks. Earl never asked Harold what bus he was meeting. Harold had noticed this early and had been grateful in a way he did not examine. Plenty of people, in a hundred and some Tuesdays, had asked. The answer was not a secret. It was just long, and it belonged to the night hours, and Earl seemed to understand that some cargo a man carries himself, the way the passengers carried their own bags down out of the buses, and nobody offered, and nobody was offended.

Harold got a coffee from the machine and sat down and opened his paperback to where the receipt was tucked. He read the same pages some nights two and three times. The reading was not the point of the book. The book was a reason to have his hands still and his eyes mostly down, so that a man coming through the doors would not feel watched the second he arrived, and so that Harold would only have to look up at the sounds that mattered: the doors, the gate counter, a bus sighing down outside in the lane.

Danny was 47 now, if Harold's arithmetic and the years were both behaving. He had been a big baby and a tall teenager and a man who walked with his head a little forward, like he was always arriving somewhere slightly late and had made his peace with it. He had worked warehouses — loading, driving a forklift, a stretch as a lead man with a radio on his belt — and he had married a woman named Carla in a courthouse ceremony Harold and Ruth had driven down to Macon for, and for a while there had been phone calls on Sundays and a grandson Harold had met exactly twice, a serious baby with Danny's forehead.

Then the pieces had come off in the order these things usually go. The job first — the warehouse cut a shift, and the lead men went with it. The marriage second — Harold did not know whose fault, and had learned not to ask a question whose answer would only be one side's weather. The apartment last. Danny had moved in with a friend from the warehouse, then out of there, then somewhere Harold's information ran out, the way a road runs out of pavement. The calls got shorter and further apart. The number changed twice. Ruth died in the second of those years, in March, and Danny came to the funeral in a borrowed jacket and stood with his hands in his pockets and left before the meal, saying he had to get back, though nobody at the funeral could have said back to what.

The last call had come two years ago, in April, on a Tuesday. Danny's voice had been cheerful in the thin way that means a man is holding the cheer up with both hands. He was riding with a friend who hauled loads up and down the coast, he said. Seeing the country. Sleeping in the truck some nights and liking it more than he expected. And then he had said the sentence Harold's Tuesdays were built out of: We come through Atlanta some Tuesdays, Dad. Right through that station on Forsyth. One of these times I'll just get off and surprise you.

Three weeks after that, the number stopped working. Not changed — Harold had called enough wrong numbers in his life to know the difference. This one rang into a silence and then a recording said it was no longer in service, in the voice of a woman who had never met anybody.

He had called it every day for a month. Then every Sunday. Then he stopped calling it and started coming here instead, on Tuesdays, at eleven, and he was aware — he was not a fool, whatever else the years had made him — that this was not a plan with any machinery in it. It was a seat in a room Danny had mentioned once. But a man goes where the last word was said. That is not foolishness. That is the only map most people ever get.

The night went the way the nights went. A bus boarded for Jacksonville a little after twelve, and the room thinned. A young woman with a child asleep across two seats fed the vending machine dollar bills it kept refusing, until Earl came and flattened the bill on the edge of the machine with his palm, the way you smooth a thing that has been folded too long, and it took. Harold read his pages. At two, Earl went down the rows with the mop. Coming back up the room, he stopped at the bench by the back wall and took a yellow umbrella off the arm of it — a bright, cheap, cheerful thing that had come in over the weekend, on somebody's arrival or somebody's departure — and hung it on the hook by the back door, where the room's left-behind things waited out their owners. Nobody had claimed it yet. The coffee from the machine was bad in a way Harold had made his peace with, the way you make peace with weather in a place you have chosen to live. He got a second one at two-thirty and held it to warm his hands. Standing still is not what made the room cold; sitting was. A room you sit in all night cools you from the chair up.

A boy — not a boy, a young man, but at 79 the word covers a multitude — came and stood at the end of Harold's row with his phone in his hand and the look of a person whose information has run out. "Sir, you know when the Athens bus goes?"

"Three-thirty," Harold said. "Gate two. It boards quiet, so you listen for Earl, not the ceiling. The ceiling'll lie to you."

"Yes sir. Thank you." The young man went and sat where he could see gate two, and Harold went back to his pages with the small, plain satisfaction of a man who is of use in a place. A hundred and some Tuesdays taught a person a station the way a route taught you a city: not the map of it, but the habits of it — which announcement to trust, which machine took a dollar kindly, which hour the room belonged to the sleepers and which to the travelers.

Once, months back, he had asked Earl why he wrote the night's buses out by hand when they were on the board above his head and on every phone in the room. Earl had considered the question with the seriousness it didn't quite deserve. "Board's been wrong," he said. "Screen in the office's been wrong. Pencil knows it can be wrong. Writes smaller because of it." Harold had thought about that answer more than once since, on the drive home. He had come to think it was the truest thing anybody had said in this room, including anything he had said himself.

He watched the doors the way he always watched them, which is to say out of the habit of his eyes rather than any hope with a date on it. Hope, at 79, is not a fire. It is a pilot light. It stays lit because turning it off would take a decision, and Harold had not made that decision, and the Tuesdays were how he didn't make it.

The 3:15 came in from New York four minutes late, and the room changed the way it always changed for that bus — people rising out of the seats and off the walls, the line at the restroom, the guard moving to the middle of the floor with his hands behind his back. Harold looked up from his book with the rest of them, out of the same habit, and did his one slow pass across the arriving faces, near to far, the way he had done it a hundred and some times, expecting nothing, which is the only way a man can keep doing a thing like that.

A man came through the doors with a duffel slung across his body, head forward, walking like he was arriving somewhere slightly late. The bag was green canvas, old, and somebody had wrapped the strap in a band of silver tape where it crossed his chest, the way you fix a thing you intend to keep.

Harold knew the walk before he knew anything else. It was in his body before it reached his mind — the height of him, the grey coming into the hair under a cap pulled low, the duffel riding on the strap across the chest the way Danny had carried every bag since he was old enough to carry one, one hand up holding the strap where it crossed, and the head forward, that forward lean, like the man was already reading the room ahead of the room. He was the right age. He was the right size. Atlanta at 3:19 in the morning does not offer a man many explanations, and it offered Harold exactly one.

He stood up.

He did not decide to stand up. He was standing, with his paperback still in his hand and his heart going in a way that had its own opinion about his age, and then he was crossing the room between the rows, and the man was walking toward the vending machines with his eyes on the lit front of them, the way a man does who has been on a bus nine hours and is thinking about coffee and nothing else in the world. Harold got close enough to see the cap had a word on it he couldn't read. Close enough to see the jaw. He said, or meant to say, his son's name.

The man turned at the sound of somebody near him, and he was not Danny.

He was not anybody Harold had ever seen. The face was narrower, and older around the eyes than Danny would be, and there was a pale scar through one eyebrow, and the whole arrangement of him — once you were close, once the walk had stopped carrying the message — was a stranger's. He looked at Harold with the mild, tired patience of a man who has been mistaken for people before, in stations, by people waiting.

"Sorry," the man said. "You need the machine?"

"No," Harold said. "No. Excuse me."

"No trouble," the man said, and turned back to the coffee lights, and that was all. No scene. The room had not even noticed. Somewhere behind Harold a gate announcement went up into the ceiling and came down muffled, and people he would never know stood up for a bus he would never ride.

He walked back to the third row and sat down in his seat and put the paperback on his knee and looked at his hands, which were doing a small, private shaking he told them to stop doing, and after a minute they mostly did.

The man from the New York bus was still in the room. He had bought his coffee and a honey bun from the machine and sat down at the end of the row beside the machines, the green duffel on the floor between his feet with the taped strap showing, and he was eating slowly, in no hurry at all to be wherever Atlanta wanted him. When Earl came down that side of the room with his clipboard, he gave the man a nod — the small one, the regular's nod, the one he gave the guard at the door — and the man nodded back without looking up from his coffee. So the room knew him, even if Harold didn't. Earl would know his name, and his business, and which nights to expect him, the way Earl knew everything in this room that could be known. Harold did not ask. A man's business in a bus station at four in the morning was his own, and the man was not Danny, and that was the whole of it. His heart settled by degrees, the way a dog settles, unwilling and then all at once. He was not embarrassed, exactly. He had not made a fool of himself in any way the room could testify to. What he felt was simpler and worse: he had seen, in one clear second, that he had been sitting in this room for two years watching for a walk and a size and a lean of the head — watching, that is, for the Danny of two years ago, who had already been a man Harold had not seen in the flesh for a year before that. Men change. Three years change them more. A beard. Weight off or on. Glasses. If Danny came through those doors on some Tuesday at 3:19 in the morning, thinner, bearded, grey all the way through, would his own father know him by the second row? Harold sat with that question the way you sit with a stone in your shoe on a long walk. You do not solve it. You learn its exact shape.

And there was the other shape, underneath it. Danny had said some Tuesdays. Not every Tuesday. And Harold was here one night in seven, in a station that ran all of them, and he was 79 years old, and the Camry's headlights were not what they had been, and neither were his eyes on the road home in the dark. He had known all of this for two years, in the way you know a thing you have decided to know later.

Earl came down the row with his clipboard a little before four, checking it against the board, and stopped where he always stopped, at the end of Harold's row, not looking at him directly, looking at the board, giving the moment its privacy.

"Earl," Harold said. "You got that pencil."

Earl took the pencil out of his shirt pocket and handed it over without asking what for, and turned the clipboard around, and Harold saw the back of it was mostly bare wood-colored board with a few numbers written small in the corners from other nights. He wrote his telephone number across the middle of it in his big, careful hand — the hand of a man who had written delivery slips in truck cabs for twenty-six years, in pencil, in the cold, and learned to make every digit unmistakable. He wrote it twice, the second time underneath, in case the first one smudged.

"If a man comes through here," Harold said, "some night. Any night. Asking after me — or a man named Daniel comes through and there's any asking to be done from your side of it. You give him that number." He handed the pencil back. "Tell him his dad sits here Tuesdays. Tell him it doesn't have to be a Tuesday."

Earl looked at the number once, the way he looked at the times on the front of the board, committing it to the only filing system he seemed to entirely trust. "I'll keep it on there," he said.

"I know you will," Harold said. "That's why it's on there and not in my pocket."

He left a little earlier than usual, while the room was still in its deep-night handful — the sleepers arranged along the walls, the guard at the other door, the woman from the New York bus asleep already with her coat folded under her head like a woman who had made this peace before. The lot was cold in the low fifties and his breath did not show. He started the Camry and let it warm the way he always did, out of respect for an old machine that had never once failed to take him home, and he drove through the empty streets with the number sitting behind him in the station, written in pencil on the back of a clipboard, in a room that was open every night of the week.

He would be back Tuesday. That was not the decision and never had been. The decision was that the waiting no longer depended on whether an old man could sit in a chair and know a face. Part of it was on the board now, where it could wait without him.
